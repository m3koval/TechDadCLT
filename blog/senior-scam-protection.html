ResourcesOlder adults / practical safety

A calmer way to handle suspicious calls and messages

Practical habits, safer account settings and a trusted-contact plan—with the older adult’s choices at the center.

Start with a pause, not an app

Scams often create urgency: a bank problem, an unexpected charge, a family emergency or someone claiming your computer is infected. A familiar voice, caller ID or convincing logo is not enough to verify the request.

End the interaction. Use a number already known to you, the number on your bank card or an independently opened official app. Do not use the link or callback number supplied by the suspicious message. Never share a verification code with an unexpected caller or pay someone demanding gift cards or cryptocurrency to fix a problem.

Make important accounts easier to protect

Review unique passwords, account recovery and explicitly enrolled two-step verification. A recovery phone number alone does not turn on two-factor authentication. Where supported and suitable, prefer a passkey or security key; keep recovery materials under the account owner’s control.

Gmail automatically filters spam. Optional Enhanced Safe Browsing adds phishing and malware checks—it is not a switch that starts basic spam filtering. Review suspicious forwarding rules and unfamiliar sessions with the account owner’s permission.

Keep important calls coming through

On supported iPhones, review Settings → Apps → Phone; screening and silencing options depend on the iOS version. Save doctors, family and other important callers first and practice reviewing voicemail. Silencing unknown callers can hide legitimate calls and does not identify every scam.

Google Call Screen is available only on supported devices and in supported regions and circumstances. Do not assume every Android phone has it. Ask your carrier about available spam controls and any separate fees.

Make family involvement a choice

An adult child paying for help does not authorize access to a parent’s private information. Agree directly on the devices, changes and information sharing. Use vendor invite-and-accept roles rather than shared credentials. Start with limited alerts and practice removing access.

Optional financial or identity monitoring can be useful for a specific need. It does not stop every payment or guarantee reimbursement. Free credit freezes can restrict access to a credit file for new credit; they are not the same as a paid credit lock, transaction alerts or protection against every kind of fraud.

If something has already happened

  1. Contact the bank or payment provider immediately. Use a known number; describe the payment and ask about reversal or protective steps. Recovery is not guaranteed.
  2. Stop the suspicious interaction. If remote access may be active, disconnect the affected device from the internet and use a separate trusted device to contact providers. Avoid deleting unfamiliar tools or messages before considering evidence preservation.
  3. Secure affected accounts. Follow official recovery steps, review sessions and reset credentials from a trusted device as appropriate. Do not send passwords or codes to a helper.
  4. Keep records and report. Save relevant messages, receipts and dates. Use FTC reporting, IdentityTheft.gov for identity-theft recovery, and IC3 for internet-crime reports. Call emergency services if there is immediate physical danger.
